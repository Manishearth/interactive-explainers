Why the pieces are not independent

A corner has three orientations, but the last corner’s twist is forced by the other seven. Likewise, the last edge’s flip is forced by the other eleven.

A quarter turn makes a 4-cycle of corners and a 4-cycle of edges. Each cycle is odd, so the two permutation parities change together.

Σ twist ≡ 0 (mod 3)
Σ flip ≡ 0 (mod 2)
sgn(corners) = sgn(edges)

The paired swap passes all three tests and is reachable, even though neither isolated swap is.

For an ordinary 3×3 cube with a fixed center frame, these conditions are both necessary and sufficient. They reduce all type-preserving reassemblies to 1/12 of the possibilities.

There are two different kinds of “reduction”

Bundle stickers into oriented pieces

This is a change of coordinates with no loss. A corner’s three stickers become one identity and one value in ℤ/3; an edge’s two stickers become one identity and one value in ℤ/2. The 48-sticker representation is valid, but it hides these rigid bundles.

Then forget the orientations

This really does lose information. Let P be the group of possible corner/edge position permutations. The projection G → P identifies states that differ only in orientation.

P = {(σ, τ) ∈ S8 × S12 : sgn σ = sgn τ}

Choose , then uncheck “Keep orientation information.” Every piece is home, so the positional view becomes identical to solved.

The information inside each fiber

Each position arrangement has 37 × 211 = 4,478,976 reachable orientation assignments above it. Above the identity permutation these form the normal subgroup N of orientation-only operations.

N ≅ (ℤ/3)7 × (ℤ/2)11
1 → N → G → P → 1

Permuting pieces also permutes where their orientation values live. That interaction gives a semidirect product:

G ≅ N ⋊ P

Not an ordinary direct product: a twist attached to one slot is carried to another by a position permutation.

Where graph theory actually enters

The diagrams above visualize an action on piece positions. A chosen face turn acts as two directed cycles plus fixed points. Adding all generators gives a labeled action graph; a token also needs orientation data. This is different from a Cayley graph, whose vertices represent entire group elements (identified here with reachable cube states), and whose edges represent face turns. The latter has about 4.3 × 1019 vertices. Merely drawing 20 dots does not turn the full cube state space into a 20-vertex Cayley graph.

Conventions, references, and how to read the diagrams

This models an ordinary 3×3 cube with unmarked centers fixed as the reference frame. Whole-cube rotations here only change the camera. The colored band on a diagram token lists that piece’s sticker colors in its current slot’s reference order; it is not a second spatial projection. The corner and edge graphs share the same underlying cube state.

Corner slot orders: URF, UFL, ULB, UBR, DFR, DLF, DBL, DRB. The orientation is the index (0, 1, or 2) of the piece’s U/D sticker in that slot’s ordered faces. Edge slot orders: UR, UF, UL, UB, DR, DF, DL, DB, FR, FL, BL, BR. The orientation is the index (0 or 1) of the piece’s first reference sticker in its current slot’s ordered faces. These are Kociemba’s cubie conventions; edge flips are not simply “whether a sticker points up.”

Arrows in “Most recent move” map source slots to destination slots. “Net permutation” arrows map each piece’s home slot to its current slot. A cycle (A → B → C) means A goes to B, B to C, C to A. Fixed points are omitted. Even a fixed piece may have nonzero orientation.
